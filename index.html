Chapter 1: The AI-Native PM Mindset

CHAPTER 1 • PHASE 1: IDEA

The AI-Native PM Mindset

From feature manager to system orchestrator

Picture a Monday planning meeting. Your engineer opens her laptop and says, "I built the first version of that export feature over the weekend, with an AI coding assistant. Want to see it?" It works. It's a bit rough, but it works. Something that would have been a three-sprint estimate last year is sitting there, running.

Everyone in the room feels the same thing: so what do I do now? If building got this cheap, what is a product manager for?

The bottleneck moved

For a long time, the scarce thing in product work was engineering capacity. That's why so much of the PM job grew around it: write the ticket, argue about priority, protect the sprint, chase the timeline. Those habits made sense when building was slow and expensive.

AI is compressing the cost of building. It isn't compressing the cost of being wrong. You can now build the wrong thing faster than ever, which is a great way to waste a quarter with a very productive-looking team.

"When building gets cheap, judgment gets expensive."

So the job shifts. Less "manager of features and timelines," more orchestrator of a system: a system made of models, code, data, rules, and people, all of which have to work together and stay trustworthy.

What an orchestrator actually does

Think of a conductor. She doesn't play every instrument, and she couldn't play them all as well as the specialists anyway. Her job is to decide what piece is being played, set the tempo, and decide who plays what and when. And she's the one who hears when something is off.

For an AI product, the same questions apply. Which parts of this experience should a model handle, and which should be plain code, a fixed rule, or a human? What does "good" sound like, and who's listening for it? When it goes off-key, who notices, and how quickly?
